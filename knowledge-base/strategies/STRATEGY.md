# The strategy, written out in full

This is one trader's day-trading strategy — Ross Cameron of Warrior Trading —
reconstructed from 257 of his instructional videos (159 hours of speech). It is
written for someone who has never day traded, so nothing is assumed.

**Read this first.** This document describes what he says he does. It is not
advice, and nothing here has been tested. He is selling a trading education
product, his results are self-reported, and most people who try this lose money.
The value of writing it down is that you now have a specific, testable set of
rules instead of a vague impression. Test them before you believe them.

---

## 1. The whole thing in one paragraph

He trades small, cheap stocks that are exploding upward *today* because of a
news story. He finds them with a scanner in the first hour or two after the
market opens. He does not try to predict a bottom or catch a falling stock — he
buys something that is already going up, waits for a small dip, and buys the
exact moment it starts going up again. He puts his stop-loss just below that
dip, so he knows his maximum loss before he clicks buy. He aims to make twice
what he is risking. He takes profit in pieces on the way up rather than trying
to sell at the top. And he stops trading for the day when he hits a preset loss
limit or gives back half his gains.

Everything below is the detail.

---

## 2. What he is actually looking for (the stock filter)

This is the most repeated idea in the entire corpus — five criteria appear
together in dozens of separate videos. A stock has to pass **all five** before
he will even look at the chart.

| # | Criterion | The number | Why he says it matters |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Price** | $2–$20 per share, sweet spot **$5–$10** | Cheap enough to move a big percentage, expensive enough to have real buyers and a tight spread |
| 2 | **Up on the day** | At least **+10%** already | The move is already proven. He is not guessing |
| 3 | **Relative volume** | At least **5x** the 50-day average | Something unusual is happening. Normal volume means no opportunity |
| 4 | **News catalyst** | A real headline today | The reason the stock is moving. Without it the move dies quickly |
| 5 | **Float** | Under **20 million shares**, prefer under **10 million** | Few shares available, so buying pressure moves the price a lot |

**"Float" explained:** the number of shares actually available to trade on the
open market. A company can have 500 million shares in existence but only 3
million floating. Small float means a small amount of buying can push the price
a long way. This is why he hunts tiny companies you have never heard of, not
Apple.

**"Relative volume" explained:** today's volume compared to what this stock
normally trades. If it usually trades 100,000 shares a day and today it has
traded 3 million, that is 30x relative volume. He treats 5x as the floor. In
practice most of his examples are far above it — 28x, 200x, 1,300x.

**"Catalyst" explained:** the news. FDA approval, an earnings surprise, a
buyout, a contract win. He is explicit that a perfect-looking chart with no news
behind it is a trap: it goes sideways and takes your money slowly. He also says
the reverse — a strong catalyst can excuse a chart that looks mediocre.

**How he finds them:** a real-time scanner that alerts him to any stock up more
than 10% with high volume. This narrows roughly 8,000 listed stocks to about 50
candidates, then to a watchlist of 3–5 he actually watches. He checks the news
headline the moment a stock alerts.

### Things he refuses to trade

- Stocks under $1 (they trade in fractions of a cent, which makes them
  unpredictable), and anything over about $20 in normal conditions.
- Stocks with no news.
- Stocks with a large float (over ~100 million shares) — they barely move.
- Stocks that popped in pre-market and then faded back down. He reads that as a
  big seller waiting at the open.
- Anything where a trading halt is likely for bad reasons (bankruptcy news).

## 3. When he trades

He trades a very short window and then stops. This is not a full-time-hours
strategy.

- **Core window: 9:30 a.m. – 11:30 a.m. Eastern**, and most of the real money is
  made in the first 30–60 minutes.
- His personal window including pre-market: **7:00 a.m. – 11:00 a.m. Eastern**.
  He has said this is based on analysing over 24,000 of his own trades.
- **He skips the first ~5 minutes** (9:30–9:35). Price swings are too wide to
  set a tight stop.
- **After 10:30 a.m. the market cools.** Volume dries up, moves get choppy, and
  he says his accuracy falls off through the day from decision fatigue.
- **He does not trade the afternoon.** He closes the charts.

Pre-market (4:00 a.m.–9:30 a.m.) and after-hours (4:00 p.m.–8:00 p.m.) have a
practical restriction worth knowing: most brokers only accept **limit orders**
outside 9:30–4:00. No market orders, no stop orders. Your stop has to be a
mental one you execute manually.

## 4. The chart setup

Simple. He is emphatic that beginners should not add more.

**Charts:** a 1-minute candlestick chart for entries, a 5-minute chart for the
overall shape, and the daily chart to see where big resistance levels sit.

**Indicators — all of them:**

| Tool | What it is | How he uses it |
|---|---|---|
| **9 EMA** | 9-period exponential moving average | The line pullbacks should bounce off in a strong uptrend. First profit target |
| **20 EMA** | 20-period moving average | Second support level. Losing it is a warning |
| **200 MA** | 200-period moving average (daily chart) | A major resistance level, especially on the daily |
| **VWAP** | Volume-weighted average price | The "fair value" line for the day. He buys above it, not below it |
| **MACD** | Momentum indicator, settings **12 / 26 / 9** | A traffic light. Positive = allowed to buy. Negative = do not buy long |
| **Level 2** | The order book — who is bidding and offering | Shows a big seller sitting above you |
| **Time & sales** | The live feed of executed trades ("the tape") | Green = trades hitting the ask (buyers in control) |

**Do not change the MACD settings.** He says custom settings produce false
signals and, more importantly, everyone else is watching the default.

**Candlestick basics you need**, because he reads price action directly:

- **Green candle** — closed higher than it opened. Buyers won that minute.
- **Red candle** — closed lower. Sellers won.
- **Topping tail** — a long wick sticking out the top. Price went up, then got
  pushed back down. Sellers are there. Warning sign.
- **Doji** — opens and closes at nearly the same price. Indecision. At the top
  of a big run, a warning.
- **Hammer** — long wick out the bottom, small body. Price got sold off and
  bought back. Bullish at the bottom of a dip.

## 5. The core setup: the micro pullback

This is the trade. Everything else is a variation of it.

**The shape:** a stock is running up. It pauses and drops slightly for one to
three candles on lighter volume. Then it starts going up again.

**The entry rule, stated identically in dozens of videos:**

> **Buy the first candle that makes a new high after the pullback.**

That is the whole trigger. Not the pause itself, not the bottom of the dip. You
wait for the price to exceed the high of the previous candle, which is proof
that buyers came back, and you buy that moment.

**What makes a good pullback versus a bad one:**

| Good | Bad |
|---|---|
| Small — a few cents, not a collapse | Deep — gives back most of the move |
| **Volume drops** during the dip | Volume *increases* during the dip (real selling) |
| Holds above the 9 EMA | Breaks the 9 EMA and 20 EMA |
| Holds above VWAP | Falls below VWAP |
| MACD still positive | MACD has crossed negative |
| Red candles are small | A large red candle on heavy volume |

He calls the ideal case a "trifecta": the pullback lands on a **whole or
half-dollar price level** ($7.00, $7.50), **on the 9 EMA**, on **light volume**,
all at once.

**The bull flag** is the same idea on a bigger timeframe: a sharp move up (the
"flagpole"), two to three candles of sideways drifting (the "flag"), then a
break above the flag's high. That break is the entry. It is the single
most-discussed named pattern in the corpus.

**Which pullback to take:** the **first and second** pullbacks of a move. He is
consistent that the **third pullback usually fails** and is where the move ends.
He also prefers the first or second bull flag *of the day*, generally inside the
first hour. Flags later in the day are weaker.

### The rule that keeps you out of most bad trades

> **MACD positive = green light. MACD negative = do not buy long.**

He states this as absolute. If the MACD has crossed below its signal line, he
will not take a long entry no matter how good the chart looks. He calls trading
before the cross the "front side" of the move and after it the "back side," and
says beginners lose money on the back side.

## 6. The stop-loss

This is not optional and it is not a feeling. It is a specific price you decide
**before** you click buy.

> **The stop goes at the low of the pullback. Never wider.**

If you buy the candle that broke above the pullback, and the pullback's lowest
price was $6.90, your stop is $6.89. If the stock trades there, you are out.

**Why this specific placement:** it is the closest logical level. If the stock
falls back below the bottom of its own pullback, the pattern you bought has
failed — there is nothing left to be right about. And because it is close, your
loss per share is small, which is what makes the whole risk-reward maths work.

**The consequence he stresses:** if the pullback was large, the stop is far
away, which means the trade is too risky — either take fewer shares or skip it.
**A big pullback is a reason not to trade, not a reason to use a wider stop.**

**Other stop levels he uses**, in order of preference:

1. Low of the pullback (the default).
2. Just below a whole or half-dollar level, if you entered on a break of one.
3. Just below a clear support level or the 9 EMA.
4. Below VWAP — if the stock loses VWAP, he treats the setup as dead.

**Moving the stop:** once the trade goes in your favour (roughly 10 cents, or
after you have sold part of the position), he moves the stop **up to break-even**
— your entry price. From that point you cannot lose money on the trade. He moves
it up further as the stock climbs, but he **never moves a stop down** to give a
losing trade more room, and he **never adds shares to a losing position**.

**"Breakout or bailout":** if the trade does not work almost immediately, he
gets out. He does not sit and hope. Small, fast losses are the point.

## 7. Taking profit

**The minimum ratio, stated everywhere in the corpus:**

> **Only take the trade if you can make at least 2x what you are risking.**

If your stop is 10 cents below your entry, you need a realistic path to 20 cents
of upside. If you cannot see it, do not take the trade.

**Why 2:1 matters so much** — this is the arithmetic that makes the strategy
work, and it is worth understanding properly:

| Your ratio | Win rate needed just to break even |
|---|---|
| 1:1 | 50% |
| 1.5:1 | ~41% |
| **2:1** | **33%** |
| 3:1 | 25% |

At 2:1 you can be **wrong two times out of three and still not lose money**. His
stated targets are 50%+ accuracy, ideally 65–75%. If he is anywhere near that
with a 2:1 ratio, the maths is strongly positive. This is why he obsesses over
tight stops: the stop size is the denominator of that ratio, and shrinking it
does more for you than picking better stocks.

**The first profit target: a retest of the high of the day.** This is the
single most-repeated target in the corpus. The stock has already been at that
price once, so it is a realistic place to reach.

**Other targets he uses:** the next whole or half-dollar level ($8.00, $8.50),
the 9 EMA on a bounce, VWAP, and on the daily chart the 200 MA.

**Scaling out — how he actually sells.** He does not sell everything at once.

1. **Sell half at the first target** (often around 15–20 cents of profit).
2. **Move the stop on the rest to break-even.** The trade is now risk-free.
3. **Sell another quarter** at the next level up.
4. Let the last piece run with a trailing stop until an exit signal appears.

He is explicit about the psychology here: he changed his sell hotkey from "sell
full position" to "sell half" specifically to force himself to hold winners
longer.

**Exit signals — get out when you see these**, regardless of your target:

- **The first candle that makes a new low** (the mirror image of the entry rule).
- A **large topping tail** — a big upper wick showing sellers overwhelmed buyers.
- A **high-volume red candle**.
- **MACD crosses negative.**
- **A large sell order appears on Level 2** blocking the price above you.
- The **tape turns red**, or buying simply stops and price stalls.
- **Green candles getting progressively smaller** — the move is running out.

His summary phrasing: *"exit when the stock is no longer obvious."*

**Realistic size of a win.** He repeatedly frames the goal as **15–20 cents per
share**, not home runs. That sounds trivial until you multiply it by share size,
which is the next section.

## 8. Position sizing — where the money actually comes from

This is the part beginners get backwards. **Your risk is not the amount of money
you spend on the shares. Your risk is the distance from your entry to your
stop, times the number of shares.**

Example. You buy 1,000 shares at $6.00. That costs $6,000 of buying power. But
your stop is at $5.90. Your actual risk is:

```
1,000 shares × $0.10 (entry − stop) = $100 at risk
```

Not $6,000. This is the single most important calculation in the strategy, and
it works in reverse — which is how you decide your share size:

```
shares = (what you're willing to lose) ÷ (entry price − stop price)
```

Willing to lose $100, stop is 10 cents away → **1,000 shares**. Same $100, but
the stop is 25 cents away → **400 shares**. Tighter stop, bigger position, same
risk. This is why he takes large share sizes on tight-stop setups and small
sizes when the pullback is wide.

**Suggested risk per trade:** he cites **2% of your account** as a maximum, and
elsewhere 3–5%. On a $1,000 account that is $20–$50 per trade. He also suggests
a flat $50 per trade for a first month.

**How he scales up over time — deliberately slowly:**

```
100 shares  →  200  →  400  →  800  →  and up
```

with the rule: **increase share size before increasing the number of trades.**
Get good at one trade a day with more size, rather than ten trades with little.

The reason is pure multiplication. The strategy makes ~20 cents per share, so:

| Share size | Profit at $0.20/share |
|---|---|
| 100 | $20 |
| 1,000 | $200 |
| 10,000 | $2,000 |

The strategy does not change. Only the size does. And the ceiling is real: at
large size you cannot get filled without moving the price against yourself,
which is why he says percentage returns fall as the account grows.

**Two hard sizing rules:**
- **Never size up so much that fear drives your decisions.** If you cannot cut
  the loss calmly, the position is too big.
- **Size up in hot markets, size down in cold ones.** After a losing day, trade
  smaller — not bigger to win it back.

## 9. The daily rules

He treats these as more important than the setups, and most of the "mistakes"
videos are about breaking them.

**Set a maximum daily loss, and make it equal to your daily profit goal.** If
your goal is $500, your max loss is $500. When you hit it, you are done for the
day. No exceptions.

He quotes a figure worth remembering: **if you break your max-loss rule, there
is roughly an 80% chance you double that loss.**

**Walk away when any of these happen:**

1. You hit your **max daily loss**.
2. You **give back half your gains** for the day.
3. You go **green to red** — you were up on the day and are now down. Stop
   immediately.
4. You take **three losses in a row**.
5. You feel **angry, rushed, or like you need to win it back**.

**When you hit your daily goal**, size back down or stop. Do not keep pressing
with the same aggression.

**One or two good trades a day is the target**, not ten. He describes "decision
fatigue" — every additional scanner alert you evaluate degrades your judgement,
and by midday his accuracy is measurably worse.

**A "good red day" is a day where you lost money but followed every rule.** A
bad green day is one where you made money by breaking them. He is consistent
that judging yourself by discipline rather than P&L is what makes traders
survive.

## 10. The other setups, briefly

The micro pullback is the core. These appear often enough to be worth knowing,
in rough order of how much he discusses them.

**Gap and go** — a stock gaps up at the open on overnight news, pulls back to
the 9 EMA in the first few minutes, then breaks higher. The trigger is often the
break of the **pre-market high**.

**Flat top breakout** — instead of drifting down, the pullback goes sideways
with a flat ceiling (repeated rejections at the same price). The break of that
ceiling is the entry. Cleaner than a normal flag because the level is obvious.

**ABCD pattern** — a run up (A to B), a pullback (B to C), then a break above B
(the D leg). Same logic, longer timeframe. He notes the stop is further away
than on a bull flag, so it needs a bigger payoff to be worth it.

**Dip and rip / halt resumption** — when a stock rises more than 10% in under 5
minutes it gets **halted** for 5 minutes. When it resumes it often dips first,
then rips higher. He buys that dip. **Rule of thumb: trade the first halt, trade
the second, do not trade the third or beyond.**

**Whole and half-dollar levels** — price gets sticky at $5.00, $5.50, $6.00.
He buys the break of these levels with the stop just underneath, and takes
partial profit into the next one.

**Reversal trading** — buying a stock that has fallen a long way and is
bouncing. He does this far less and warns beginners off it. His stated version:
wait for 10–15 consecutive candles in one direction, then take the first
candle that reverses. **Short selling** he does rarely and warns about heavily —
losses are theoretically unlimited and a single gap-up after a halt can erase
months of profit.

## 11. If you are starting from zero

This is the sequence he lays out, condensed.

1. **Learn to read a candlestick chart** without any indicators. Green, red,
   wicks, what a pullback looks like. This comes before everything else.

2. **Paper trade in a simulator.** Same rules, same share sizes, no real money.
   You are looking for a stretch of consistent green results, not one good day.
   Be aware simulators fill orders unrealistically — a fill you get in a sim is
   not proof you would get it live.

3. **Go live with tiny size.** 50 or 100 shares. The point is not the money, it
   is finding out how you behave when the money is real. Nearly everyone trades
   worse the first time it counts.

4. **One trade per day, one setup.** The micro pullback. Nothing else. Track
   whether you followed your rules — not whether you made money.

5. **Scale share size only after consistency.** 100 → 200 → 400 → 800, and only
   after a stretch of profitable days at the current size.

**Set up hotkeys early.** This strategy needs orders placed in under a second.
He recommends starting with exactly three: **buy**, **sell full position**,
**cancel all orders** — then adding sell-half and sell-quarter later. Trying to
click through an order ticket while a stock is moving will cost you more than
any setup will make.

**The PDT rule.** In the US, with under **$25,000** in a margin account, you are
limited to **three day trades per rolling five business days**. Under that
threshold you physically cannot trade this strategy daily. Options: a cash
account (but you wait for settlement), an offshore broker, or building the
account to $25,000 first.

## 12. What this document does not tell you

Be honest about the gaps. They matter more than the rules.

- **None of it is verified.** Every number here comes from someone talking on
  YouTube. No backtest, no trade log, no independent audit. The corpus contains
  983 statements flagged as unverifiable claims.

- **The results are unrepresentative by construction.** He is famous because he
  succeeded. You are not hearing from the people who followed identical rules
  and lost. His own disclaimers say results are not typical, and Warrior
  Trading's own regulatory settlement history is worth reading before you treat
  any of it as a track record.

- **His edge may not be the rules.** Speed of execution, expensive tools,
  years of screen time, and a very large account are all part of how he trades
  and none of them transfer by reading.

- **The market changes.** These videos span years. Low-float momentum behaved
  differently in 2017, 2021, and today. Rules that worked in one regime can stop
  working without announcing it.

- **The transcripts are auto-generated captions.** No punctuation, and ticker
  symbols and numbers are frequently misheard. Any specific figure here should be
  checked against the source video before you rely on it. The per-video summaries
  in `../summaries/` carry timestamps back to where each rule was stated.

**The correct next step is not to trade this. It is to test it** — code the
filter, pull historical data for stocks that passed it, and measure what the
entry and stop rules would actually have returned. That is what the rest of this
repository is for.
